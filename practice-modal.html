Buying tickets to a concert, game, or show should feel exciting. But it can quickly turn into a headache when a transfer email is late, the mobile ticket won't load at the gate, the event date changes, or the seat you get isn't the one you paid for.

Here's a practical, no-jargon guide to how ticket resale works, what to check before paying, and how to deal with the five most common issues — while avoiding scams and unnecessary frustration.

How Ticket Resale Actually Works

StubHub is a marketplace — a middle ground where sellers list tickets and buyers shop for them. That means the seller, not the platform, controls most of the details: the price, the seat, and sometimes the timing of the delivery.

Because of that, tickets usually arrive in one of a few ways:

  • Mobile transfer — the seller sends the ticket to your email or phone, and you may need to accept it through a venue or team app.
  • Instant download — a PDF or barcode becomes available as soon as the order is confirmed.
  • Delayed release — the ticket only becomes available days or hours before the event, once the venue enables transfers.

Prices on resale markets can be higher or lower than face value. That's normal — they reflect demand, seat location, timing, and what the seller decides to charge. Always check the full checkout total, not just the listed price, because fees and taxes can change the number significantly.

Checks to Make Before You Buy

The single most important step is making sure you're on the real website. Type the address yourself or use a bookmark you trust. A page using a familiar brand name is not automatically approved by that brand.

Once you're on a legitimate page, slow down and verify these things before you hit pay:

  • The event, city, venue, date, and start time
  • Section, row, and seat numbers — and whether multiple seats are actually together
  • How the ticket will be delivered (mobile transfer, app, PDF, etc.)
  • The currency, service fees, taxes, and final total
  • Age restrictions, accessibility rules, or restricted-view warnings

Take a screenshot of the listing and the confirmation page. It only takes a few seconds, and if something goes wrong later, you'll have proof of exactly what was advertised.

Screenshots of barcodes won't always work

Many venues now use rotating or encrypted mobile barcodes. A screenshot of a barcode may be rejected at the gate. Always use the ticket inside the official app or wallet specified by the venue.

The 5 Most Common Ticket Problems (and How to Handle Them)

Here are the issues people run into most often — and what to actually do about each one.

1 The ticket transfer hasn't arrived

A delayed transfer doesn't always mean the order failed. Many tickets can't be released until the venue opens transfers — sometimes only days before the event.

Start by checking the estimated delivery date in your order. Then search your inbox, spam folder, promotions tab, and trash — transfer emails often land in the wrong place. Confirm your account email is correct and up to date.

If the promised deadline has passed, use the order-help option inside the official StubHub account or app. Keep your order number private and share it only through a verified support channel.

2 The transfer arrived, but the ticket won't show up

Open the transfer link from the same email address it was sent to. If you're signed into a different account, the ticket may not appear.

You may also need an account with the venue, team, or primary ticket provider (like Ticketmaster or AXS). Install their official app, update it, and sign out and back in.

Before asking for help, save the exact error message and take a screenshot. It speeds up every support conversation.

3 The event was postponed, rescheduled, or canceled

Each situation has its own rules. A postponed event usually keeps the same tickets valid for the new date. A canceled event may trigger a refund or a credit — it depends on the organizer and the marketplace.

Compare the notice in your StubHub account with the venue or organizer's official announcement. If you booked travel or a hotel separately, contact those providers directly — they have their own cancellation policies.

4 The ticket is rejected at the gate

Make sure you're using the latest ticket in the official app — not an old screenshot or a barcode you saved days ago.

Ask venue staff to explain the rejection. If possible, get written confirmation or a photo of the error message. Then contact the marketplace through the official order-help channel as soon as possible.

Never post the barcode, transfer link, or security code publicly while you're sorting this out. Someone else could use it before you do.

5 The section, row, or quantity is wrong

Compare what you received with the original listing and order confirmation. Take clear screenshots showing the difference.

Don't transfer, resell, or modify the ticket while the issue is being reviewed — that can complicate the resolution. Follow any instructions from official support closely.

A Few Notes for Sellers

If you're selling tickets, the most common mistake is being vague. Enter the correct event, date, quantity, section, row, and seat details. Disclose anything that could affect the buyer's experience — restricted views, accessibility rules, age limits, or parking-only passes.

Never list a ticket you can't actually deliver by the promised deadline. After a sale, follow the platform's transfer steps exactly, verify the recipient email before sending, and keep proof that the transfer was sent, claimed, or accepted.

Also worth remembering: resale laws and tax rules vary by location. If you're selling frequently, it's worth understanding the rules that apply to you.

Spotting Fake Support and Ticket Scams

Be very cautious of any page, ad, or popup that creates urgency — especially wording like "call immediately," "account blocked," or "only support number." These are classic scam patterns.

An independent site should clearly say who runs it. It should never pretend to be the official StubHub help desk.

Red flags to walk away from

Anyone asking for gift cards, cryptocurrency, banking passwords, one-time security codes, or remote access to your phone or computer. Real support never asks for these.

Don't share order numbers, transfer links, email addresses, payment info, or ticket barcodes in comments or public forums. If you notice a fraudulent charge on your card, contact your card issuer directly using the number printed on the back of the card.

The Short Version

Most ticket problems come down to timing, email confusion, or a mismatch between the listing and the delivered ticket. Slow down before paying, save your screenshots, and use the official order-help channel when something goes wrong.

And if a "support" page asks for gift cards, remote access, or passwords — close the tab. That's not support, that's a scam.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is this an official StubHub support website?
No. This is an independent educational page written for general consumer guidance. It doesn't represent StubHub, access accounts, process orders, or issue refunds.
Why hasn't my mobile ticket arrived yet?
Ticket-release times vary by event and venue. Some sellers can only release tickets days or hours before the event. Check the estimated delivery date in your order, then look in spam, promotions, and trash folders. If the deadline has passed, use the official order-help channel.
Can I get into an event with a screenshot of my ticket?
Not always. Many venues now use rotating or protected mobile barcodes. Use the ticket inside the official app or mobile wallet the venue or provider specifies — not a screenshot.
What should I do if a transfer link doesn't work?
Double-check the recipient email address, try the official app, install any available updates, and save the exact error message. Then use the help option attached to your order.
Are resale prices the same as face value?
Not necessarily. Resale prices can be higher or lower than face value and change based on demand, seat location, timing, and seller decisions.
What happens if an event is postponed?
Tickets usually stay valid for the new date. Read the marketplace notice and the organizer's official announcement before assuming you're entitled to a refund.
How can I tell if a support page is a scam?
Warning signs include unclear ownership, copied branding, pressure to call a number immediately, and requests for remote access, gift cards, passwords, crypto, or one-time security codes.
Can an independent site use the word "StubHub"?
A brand name can be mentioned for genuine informational or comparison purposes — but it shouldn't be used to impersonate the brand or confuse readers. If you're unsure about your own use, consult a legal professional.
